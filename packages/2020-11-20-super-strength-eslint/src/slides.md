<!--
ESLint and other forms of static analysis are a key part of what I think of as the ideal “bug acquisition funnel”. Think of all the possible strategies you can take to reduce bugs in your end product as parts of a long process that starts from your application existing in the first place, stretches through the development process, and ends with it in your users’ hands.
The funnel starts by taking all bugs that could be introduced to your code and hopefully reduces them by a fair amount using developer foresight and good application architectures: putting prior thought into structuring your application is the first step to ensuring it works well.
Static analysis comes into play at development time by helping you catch inconsistencies and potential bugs in real time as you type or build your software. This is different from dynamic analysis, such as unit or end-to-end tests, that must run on at least partially-working versions of your code.
For team projects, one hopes you can have peer review to get more eyes on your code and catch bugs or other faults before merging. Once you do release, you might have some forms of automatic telemetry that report crashes or other unexpected behavior in the wild. If all else fails, I would certainly hope you have some line of communication with consumers of your application so you can receive user feedback.
It is incredibly rare for a software application to completely catch and fix all its bugs, so this funnel assumes some are left over even with all the steps. But, each can reduce the remaining bug numbers, which alone is a strong motivator to consider all of them on your long-lived projects.
Some necessary caveats: this funnel diagram is certainly not to scale with any precision, and all software projects are different. If you skip some parts of the funnel, you might not be missing much – or you might be adding to the bug load later parts of the funnel have to catch. Your weekend side project might not need the same level of scrutiny as the Mars rover.
-->
