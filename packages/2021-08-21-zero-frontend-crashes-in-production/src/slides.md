<!--
Think of all the possible strategies you can take to reduce bugs in your end product as parts of a long process that starts from your application existing in the first place, stretches through the development process, and ends with it in your users’ hands. In product terms, we’d call this an acquisition funnel. I like to call it the “bug acquisition funnel”.
The funnel starts by taking all bugs that could be introduced to your code and hopefully reduces them by a fair amount using developer foresight and good application architectures: putting prior thought into structuring your application is the first step to ensuring it works well.
Static analysis comes into play at development time by helping you catch inconsistencies and potential bugs in real time as you type or build your software. This is different from dynamic analysis, such as unit or end-to-end tests, that must run on at least partially-working versions of your code.
For team projects, one hopes you can have peer review to get more eyes on your code and catch bugs or other faults before merging. Once you do release, you might have some forms of automatic telemetry that report crashes or other unexpected behavior in the wild. If all else fails, I would certainly hope you have some line of communication with consumers of your application so you can receive user feedback.
It is incredibly rare for a software application to completely catch and fix all its bugs, so this funnel assumes some are left over even with all the steps. But, each can reduce the remaining bug numbers, which alone is a strong motivator to consider all of them on your long-lived projects. It would be foolish to decry any individual portion of the funnel as ineffective for not catching all bugs on its own. You won't perfect your application runtime with, say, just forethought and tests - but that doesn't mean we wouldn't apply both!
For an analogy, when I get to experience the joy that is New York Fall in a month, you can bet I'll be wearing a scarf every day.
Not because it staves off cold on its own, but because it is a wonderful addition to most autumn outfits that does help keep me warm.
Some necessary caveats: this funnel diagram is certainly not to scale with any precision, and all software projects are different. If you skip some parts of the funnel, you might not be missing much – or you might be adding to the bug load later parts of the funnel have to catch. Your weekend side project might not need the same level of scrutiny as the Mars rover.

Let’s run through a hypothetical post-mortem on a bug crash being reported in production. On the left side, under our happy angel, I’ll simulate the happy path of properly asking system questions in a blameless manner. On the right side, under our unhappy devil, I’ll simulate the dead ends of rudely blaming the first thing that comes to mind.
To start, the worst thing you can do is immediately blame the immediate author of a bug. Yes, developers should take responsibility for what they push – I have yet to meet a developer who doesn’t mentally punish themselves for pushing bugs to production. At this stage of the post-mortem we don’t even know why the bug has happened yet; it might be the developer had no way of knowing it would have happened. Let’s dig in: why are the crashes happening?
Suppose it’s because the frontend supports a type of user that the API does not. Now, again, we shouldn’t jump to blaming anybody for this feature gap: neither the frontend for jumping ahead nor the backend for not. Why are those users not compatible with the API?
(a corollary that very frequently comes out of post-mortems could also be asking why this particular situation wasn’t covered by testing – a great question, and perhaps something for the team to follow up on)
Let’s say the API understands users differently and has a different kind of support for this slice of user. The folks working on the API set things up differently from the folks working on the frontend. Why aren’t they sharing user needs? Let’s not jump to blaming management, why is there that communication gap?
